In 1982, a grant of fifteen thousand dollars underwrote a student conference on federalism at Yale Law School. The speakers included Robert Bork and Antonin Scalia. The organisation that grew from that conference, the Federalist Society, now has chapters at every accredited law school in the country, more than seventy thousand members, and, by 2024, six of the nine sitting Supreme Court justices among its members and affiliates.[^1]

The trajectory behind the conference is just as instructive. Constitutional originalism began as a single law review article, Bork's in 1971, which most of the legal academy dismissed. It was cultivated deliberately, in what one historian calls an *academy in exile* inside the Meese Justice Department, and it reached institutional dominance roughly forty years after the article appeared. The scholars who study this history converge on one explanation, stated most precisely by Steven Teles, which is that ideas become power only when they acquire networks to share them, organisations to connect them to problems, and patrons to sustain both. Scholarship came first, institutions followed, and dominance arrived a generation later.[^2]

## The Missing Starting Point

This archive exists because the mutual aid tradition has never had even the first step of that sequence. The practice is 240 years old, and its infrastructure of meaning is nearly empty, because there is no comprehensive citable history, no curriculum with published reach, no museum, and no reference work that a teacher or journalist or judge can pull from the shelf. The gap runs to orders of magnitude. The displacement succeeded partly for that reason, since the exclusionary account of American identity prevailed less because it was truer than because it was the only account that had been written down, funded, and distributed.

So the archive is itself a move in the contest it documents. Its purpose is to place the mutual aid tradition in the record as credible, citable civic knowledge. That record includes the Free African Society and the *Narrative* of 1794, the lodges and the *landsmanshaftn* and the Six Companies, the citizenship schools and the breakfast programmes and the buddy systems, the boundary that followed exclusion rather than principle, and the machinery, legal and discursive, that has kept all of it out of the national story. Every page rests on named, checkable sources, because a contestation document that cannot survive hostile checking is no use to anyone.[^3]

## The Honest Timescale

A single document does not compete with a curriculum enrolling 1.7 million students, and this one does not claim to. The originalism precedent cuts both ways, and this archive accepts both edges. Infrastructure can be built from a starting point as small as one article and one conference, and the building takes decades. What a starting point provides is the thing without which no infrastructure can begin, which is the knowledge base, assembled and defensible.

![Title page of the 1794 Narrative](/images/a1/narrative-1794-title.jpg "The title page of Jones and Allen's 1794 Narrative, the tradition's first contestation document, and the model for this one. © Public domain, via Internet Archive https://archive.org/details/b32886561")

The tradition itself has always understood this. Jones and Allen published their *Narrative* in 1794 because the record was being falsified in real time. The AIDS generation built its oral history archives and named a political history *Let the Record Show*. The historians of women's care networks recovered the practice diary by diary, against the grain of archives that never thought it worth keeping. Writing it down is the tradition's oldest act of self-defence, performed each time by people who understood that care that goes unrecorded goes unremembered, and care that goes unremembered can be displaced without anyone noticing the loss. This archive is that act, performed again, for the whole tradition at once.[^4]
