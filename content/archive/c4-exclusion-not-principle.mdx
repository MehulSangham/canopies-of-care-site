In 1784, a Black Bostonian named Prince Hall received a Masonic charter for African Lodge No. 459. It came from the Grand Lodge of England. He and fourteen other free Black men had been Masons for nearly a decade, initiated by a British military lodge, and no American lodge would grant them a charter of their own. The founding document of Black freemasonry in America had to cross the Atlantic because it could not cross the street.[^1]

![Portrait traditionally identified as Prince Hall](/images/c4/prince-hall.jpg "A portrait traditionally identified as Prince Hall, founder of African Lodge No. 459. No authenticated likeness from his lifetime survives. © Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Prince_hall_portrait.jpg")

That detail is the key to the whole pattern of bounded societies. Nearly every ethnically bounded mutual aid institution in the tradition has the same shape in its founding story. A door closed first, and the society formed on the other side of it. The Free African Society organised in 1787 among people excluded from Philadelphia's white congregations and denied ordinary insurance. The Grand United Order of Odd Fellows was chartered from England in 1843 after the American order refused Black applicants. The *landsmanshaftn* multiplied among Jewish immigrants facing hostility from institutions that would not have them. The Chinese family associations and the Six Companies did their work under the Chinese Exclusion Act, serving a population that federal law had singled out by name.[^2]

![First page of the Chinese Exclusion Act, 1882](/images/c4/chinese-exclusion-act-1882.jpg "The first page of the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, the federal law under which the Chinese family associations and the Six Companies did their work. © Public domain, National Archives, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chineseexclusionact.JPG")

## The Mechanism, Stated Plainly

None of this requires reading between the lines. The Historical Society of Pennsylvania, custodian of much of the fraternal archive, states the mechanism directly in its own teaching materials:

> Until the 1890s, older American societies such as the Freemasons or Knights of Pythias welcomed people from different backgrounds. By the turn of the twentieth century, however, a rise in anti-immigrant sentiment and racist attitudes created a bias against allowing new immigrants and African Americans to join these established fraternals. In order to meet their social, psychological, and economic needs, immigrants and African Americans were forced to form their own mutual aid associations, fraternal groups, and social clubs.

The white orders wrote the exclusion into their constitutions, adopting whites-only membership clauses that the Black Odd Fellows, the Colored Knights of Pythias, and the Improved Benevolent and Protective Order of Elks were each founded to route around. The boundedness of the resulting societies was the shape of the wall, traced from the inside.[^3]

## Testing the Two Explanations

The claim can be tested, because the two explanations predict different futures. If people simply prefer to care for their own kind, then the ethnic boundaries should persist wherever mutual aid persists, whatever else changes. If the boundaries were consequences of exclusion, then the practice should cross lines whenever the exclusion lifts or the situation overrides it. The history itself has run the test. In crisis, the aid crossed immediately, from 1793 onward. When the organising basis became class, the aid crossed. When founders chose the neighbourhood as the boundary, the aid crossed. And the newest generation of networks, formed in the least formally exclusionary period of American history, overwhelmingly organises by geography and need, with no ethnic term in the charter.[^4]

The memory of a tradition that only ever served its own therefore has the history backwards. The boundary around the care was drawn by exclusion rather than by the people doing the caring. What remains to be explained is the strangest fact of all, which is that a tradition this large, this old, and this well documented is almost entirely absent from the American story. That absence is not an accident.
