In October 1928, campaigning in New York, Herbert Hoover gave the American style of self-sufficiency its lasting name: rugged individualism.[^rugged] The phrase arrived a year before the crash that would test it. At the moment he spoke, on the most conservative estimate, a third of the adult men in his audience's country carried a card from a fraternal order, paying dues into common funds that hired their doctors, buried their dead, and supported their widows.[^lodges] Two accounts of what it means to belong to America were both at high tide that autumn. Only one of them had just been given a name.

This page names the other one, because the whole archive runs on the distinction between them.

## Belonging as Practice

In the first account, belonging is something people do. You are part of the community you show up for, and the proof of membership is the showing up, in dues paid, the sick visited, meals delivered, and burials attended. The Free African Society wrote this account down in 1787, in its founding articles, as a plain commitment "to support one another in sickness, and for the benefit of their widows and fatherless children."[^fas] Every institution in this archive descends from that sentence. Community, in this account, is woven. It has no fixed inside or outside, and when it weakens, the repair is more weaving.

## Belonging as Condition

In the second account, belonging is a status that precedes anything you do. Membership must be established first, by birth, by blood, by compliance, or by proof of self-sufficiency, and care flows only after the ledger of the deserving has been checked. The nation, in this account, is a container, because it has an inside and an outside, and when it weakens, the repair is a tighter boundary. The political theory underneath it is old and has a scholarly name, possessive individualism, the idea that a person is essentially the owner of himself and owes society nothing for his capacities.[^possessive]

The second account is also American. It is as old as the first, it has run just as continuously, and this archive treats it as a real tradition rather than a caricature. It wrote the exclusion laws, drew [the colour bars that made the societies look ethnic](/archive/c4-exclusion-not-principle), and supplied the moral language in which absorbing or raiding the practice could be described as progress.

## The Unequal Contest

The two accounts have never competed on equal terms, and the difference is infrastructure. The practice account lived in minute books, dues ledgers, and burial rolls, which are records that absorption scattered and raids destroyed. The condition account, from 1971 onward, was deliberately built into institutions designed to make it feel timeless, which meant think tanks, law societies, curricula, and judicial benches.[^powell] One account kept the receipts of what people did for each other. The other built the machinery that decides what the country remembers. That asymmetry, and no failure of the practice itself, is why one account reads as common sense and the other requires an archive.

## Reading the Timeline

The [landing timeline](/) carries both accounts on one spine. The green strand is the practice account in action, because every event on it is a group of people organising care for each other. The red strand is the condition account in action, and it tracks statutes, absorptions, campaigns, and the patient construction of a story. Watch the two behaviours in any crisis on the line. The container tightens. The fabric weaves wider. That pair of reflexes, repeating for two hundred and forty years, is the shortest version of the argument this archive makes at full length, beginning with [the two founding meetings of 1787](/archive/a1-founding-era).
