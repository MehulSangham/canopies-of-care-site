In 1889, Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr moved into a worn mansion on Halsted Street in Chicago's Nineteenth Ward and opened its doors to the neighbourhood, which meant, in that ward, Italians, Greeks, Russian and Polish Jews, Bohemians, Irish, and eventually Mexicans, all at once. **Hull House** ran a kindergarten, a public kitchen, English and citizenship classes, a labour museum, clubs, baths, and one of the first public playgrounds in the city, and its founding principle was residence, because the people who ran it lived there, as neighbours of the people it served. The membership rule was the address rather than the origin.[^1]

The settlement houses enter the tradition with an honest caveat attached. They were funded by the wealthy and staffed by the educated, the power inside them was never equal, and historians rightly place them at the boundary between mutual aid and philanthropy. What they demonstrate is narrower but still important. As early as the 1880s, Americans were deliberately designing care institutions whose boundary was the place rather than the group, and they were doing it in the most ethnically mixed neighbourhoods on the continent.[^2]

## Serving Whoever Lived There

The stronger examples came later, from within the tradition itself. The Black Panther breakfast tables fed every hungry child who walked in, because the programme's design covered the neighbourhood's children, full stop, and at its height the party was feeding more children daily than some state governments. The survival programmes were built by a Black organisation and rooted in Black neighbourhoods, but they were bounded by presence rather than by race, and their own documents state that design plainly.[^3]

The post-2020 networks made the same choice at city scale and said so in their founding documents. Bed-Stuy Strong's membership rule is the neighbourhood. The COVID-era networks that carried groceries to Chinatown's seniors were staffed across racial lines. On the Lower East Side, Black, Puerto Rican, and Chinese mutual aid traditions that had run side by side for a century increasingly run together, in shared buildings and shared directories. Dean Spade, the movement's most cited contemporary theorist, defines the practice by need and solidarity, with no ethnic term anywhere in the definition.[^4]

## A Choice, Made Repeatedly

Crisis, the record shows, reliably pushes mutual aid across community lines. These institutions supply the other half of the answer, because crossing the line has also been a deliberate, peacetime design decision, made in 1889, in 1969, and in 2020, whenever organisers decided that the relevant "us" was everyone in the ward. The non-ethnic form of the tradition is therefore neither a modern innovation nor an accident of emergency. It is one of the tradition's recurring shapes, and it is the shape that the newest networks overwhelmingly take.

One part of the objection nevertheless remains standing. If the practice could always cross lines, so much of its history still ran inside them, and that fact needs its own explanation.
