Between 1785 and 1812, a Maine midwife named Martha Ballard kept a daily diary: 816 deliveries, thousands of medical calls, and, woven through them, the workings of an entire economy that appears in no ledger of the period. Neighbours sitting up through the night with the sick. Women attending each other's births. Food moving between households with illness and harvest. The laying out of the dead. For over a century, historians who encountered the diary dismissed it as trivial, the repetitive jottings of a woman's routine. When Laurel Thatcher Ulrich finally read it whole in the 1980s, it won the Pulitzer Prize, because it turned out to be one of the most complete records in existence of how early American communities actually took care of themselves.[^1]

That century of dismissal is the story here. The most pervasive mutual aid system in American history is the one women have always run: birth attendance, sickroom care, meal circuits, childcare exchange, the coordination of help around every family crisis. It is mutual aid by every definition this archive uses, reciprocal, organised, and meeting needs no institution met. It is also the least documented, and the reason lies in the archives rather than the practice: what the networks left behind was coded as "women's work" rather than civic practice, and the record-keepers were not collecting it.[^2]

## Recovered Against the Grain

The record exists where historians have gone looking for it. Emily Abel reconstructed the reciprocal care networks of nineteenth-century women, the sitting up, the birth attendance, the laying out of the dead, directly from diaries like the Gillespie family's in Iowa. Anne Firor Scott documented women's benevolent associations, including Black women's associations, organising from the 1790s onward, the same decade as the Free African Society. The scholarship is real and rigorous. It is also recent, and every one of these historians describes working against the grain of an archive that never considered the material worth collecting. The thinness of the record measures what the record-keepers valued rather than how much the practice mattered.[^3]

The same invisibility has a wage dimension. The women for whom care was also employment, disproportionately Black women in domestic work, organised mutual aid inside their exclusion from labour law: hiring networks, benefit pools, training circles, and eventually a movement. Premilla Nadasen's history of domestic worker organising recovers a tradition that ran for decades in kitchens and church basements before any formal union would touch it.[^4]

## The Meal Train Runs Daily

The practice never stopped; only the recording gaps persist. Family, friend, and neighbour care remains the backbone of American childcare, larger than the formal daycare sector it quietly subsidises. Meal trains still assemble around every birth and diagnosis. Studies of low-income single mothers document dense informal networks moving food, childcare, and money between households, the same architecture Martha Ballard recorded, running on group chats instead of horseback. When the pandemic closed schools in 2020, parents assembled learning pods and childcare co-ops within weeks, and the speed was not surprising to anyone who understood that the infrastructure had been there all along.[^5]

Here sits the centre of the archive's argument about invisibility. The fraternal lodges at least left minute books and regalia; the state absorbed and erased them, but the paper survived. The maternal networks left almost no paper at all, because the people keeping civic records did not consider a meal train civic. A tradition can be erased by displacement, and it can be erased by never being written down in the first place. The pages that follow turn to the hardest question this history faces: whether all of it, lodges and societies and networks alike, was only ever each group caring for its own.[^6]
