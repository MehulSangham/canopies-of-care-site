Between 1785 and 1812, a Maine midwife named Martha Ballard kept a daily diary that recorded 816 deliveries and thousands of medical calls, and woven through those entries is the working of an entire economy that appears in no ledger of the period. Neighbours sat up through the night with the sick, women attended one another's births, food moved between households as illness and harvest required, and the dead were washed and laid out at home. For over a century, historians who encountered the diary dismissed it as trivial, treating it as the repetitive jottings of a woman's routine. When Laurel Thatcher Ulrich finally read it whole in the 1980s, her study of it won the Pulitzer Prize, because the diary turned out to be one of the most complete records in existence of how early American communities actually took care of themselves.[^1]

That century of dismissal is itself part of the history. The most pervasive mutual aid system in America has always been the one that women ran, through birth attendance, sickroom care, meal circuits, childcare exchange, and the coordination of help around every family crisis. It is mutual aid by every definition, because it was reciprocal, it was organised, and it met needs that no institution met. It is also the least documented of all the traditions, and the reason lies in the archives rather than in the practice, because what the networks left behind was coded as "women's work" rather than civic activity, and the record-keepers were not collecting it.[^2]

## Recovered Against the Grain of the Archive

The record exists where historians have gone looking for it. Emily Abel reconstructed the reciprocal care networks of nineteenth-century women, the sitting up, the birth attendance, the laying out of the dead, directly from diaries like the Gillespie family's in Iowa. Anne Firor Scott documented women's benevolent associations, including Black women's associations, organising from the 1790s onward, the same decade as the Free African Society. The scholarship is real and rigorous. It is also recent, and every one of these historians describes working against the grain of an archive that never considered the material worth collecting. The thinness of the record measures what the record-keepers valued rather than how much the practice mattered.[^3]

The same invisibility has a wage dimension. The women for whom care was also employment, who were disproportionately Black women in domestic work, organised mutual aid inside their exclusion from labour law, building hiring networks, benefit pools, training circles, and eventually a movement. Premilla Nadasen's history of domestic worker organising recovers a tradition that ran for decades in kitchens and church basements before any formal union would touch it.[^4]

## The Meal Train Runs Daily

The practice never stopped. Only the gaps in the record persisted. Family, friend, and neighbour care remains the backbone of American childcare, and it is larger than the formal daycare sector that it quietly subsidises. Meal trains still assemble around every birth and diagnosis. Studies of low-income single mothers document dense informal networks moving food, childcare, and money between households, and this is the same architecture that Martha Ballard recorded, running on group chats instead of horseback. When the pandemic closed schools in 2020, parents assembled learning pods and childcare co-ops within weeks, and the speed surprised no one who understood that the infrastructure had been there all along.[^5]

The comparison with the lodges makes the form of this erasure precise. The fraternal lodges at least left minute books and regalia, so that when the state absorbed their functions and the memory of them faded, the paper survived. The maternal networks left almost no paper at all, because the people who kept civic records did not consider a meal train civic. A tradition can be erased by displacement, and it can also be erased by never being written down in the first place. There remains a harder question, which is whether all of it, the lodges and societies and networks alike, was only ever each group caring for its own.[^6]
