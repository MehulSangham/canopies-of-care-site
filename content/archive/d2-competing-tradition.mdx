On 23 August 1971, a Virginia lawyer named Lewis Powell, two months before his nomination to the Supreme Court, sent a confidential memorandum to the US Chamber of Commerce. The **Powell Memorandum** argued that the American free enterprise system was under attack, and that business should respond by building durable infrastructure in the places where public meaning is made: campuses, textbooks, television, scholarly journals, the courts. It was a plan for constructing the machinery that produces arguments rather than for winning any single one, and much of the institutional world it called for was built within a decade, including the Heritage Foundation, founded in 1973.[^1]

The construction is one of the best-documented episodes in modern American politics. Scholars have traced how a network of foundations (Olin, Scaife, Bradley, Koch) funded think tanks, endowed law-school programmes, built the Federalist Society's judicial pipeline, and sustained a publishing and media apparatus, on the explicit theory that ideas need organisations and patrons to become common sense. The story matters to this archive because of *which* account of American identity the machinery was built to circulate: the founding documents, constitutional originalism, and individual self-reliance, presented as the whole of the civic inheritance.[^2]

## The Numbers Are Public

The civic education arm of this infrastructure publishes its own reach figures. Hillsdale College's free online curriculum reports 1.7 million enrolled students; its Constitution 101 course alone reports more than a million. Its speech digest, *Imprimis*, claims a circulation above 7.5 million. The Heritage Foundation maintains a national curriculum library of free lesson plans, primary source kits, and teaching guides on the founders and the Constitution, and conservative-funded civic institutes have been established at public universities across the country. None of these figures comes from a critic; they are the institutions' own press releases and annual reports.[^3]

Set the mutual aid tradition beside that and measure. A documented survey of mutual aid teaching materials, conducted for this project in 2026, found the following, exhaustively: one graduate seminar syllabus, one teaching guide attached to Dean Spade's 2020 book, one workshop resource page, an activist toolkit, and scattered individual lesson plans. Not one had published enrolment figures. The largest teaching resource for a civic tradition that predates the Constitution is a single professor's syllabus, against a curriculum with 1.7 million students. The asymmetry is not close; it is several orders of magnitude.[^4]

## The Empty Shelf

Here the honest boundary of the evidence should be stated. That the infrastructure exists, was deliberately built, and mass-distributes a founding-documents account of American identity is documented fact. That this *displaces* the mutual aid tradition, rather than merely outcompeting it by default, is an interpretation, though a well-grounded one: a tradition absent from every classroom, curriculum, and explainer is not one citizens can find when they go looking for what being American means. Teaching the Constitution is legitimate. Teaching it as the whole story, while the tradition of the Free African Society, the lodges, the breakfast programmes, and the buddy systems appears nowhere at all, produces exactly the invisibility this archive set out to explain.[^5]

One tradition invested in the infrastructure of meaning. The other invested in the infrastructure of care. When Americans in a crisis reach for a story about who they are, only one of the two is waiting on the shelf. The next page examines what happened when the story on the shelf was translated into a plan for the federal government.
