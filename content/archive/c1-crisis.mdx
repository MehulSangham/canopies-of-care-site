American mutual aid is often remembered, when it is remembered at all, as each group caring for its own, with Black societies for Black members and Jewish societies for Jewish members, in a history of separate solidarities rather than a shared civic tradition. The historical record complicates that memory, and the oldest complication arrived in the tradition's very first decade.

In 1793, when yellow fever killed a tenth of Philadelphia and the government of the United States fled its own capital, the Free African Society organised the nursing of the sick and the burial of the dead, and the sick and the dead were overwhelmingly white. The people whom the Constitution had excluded six years earlier carried the city that excluded them, at the cost of their own dead, and they published the record of what they had done when they were slandered for profiteering. The first documented mutual aid organisation in the country therefore crossed the colour line in its first decade, and it crossed in the direction that self-interest is least able to explain.[^1]

## The Pattern in Every Disaster

The same crossing recurs wherever catastrophe lands. Weeks after Hurricane Katrina in 2005, the **Common Ground Collective** formed in the Algiers neighbourhood of New Orleans, co-founded by Malik Rahim, a former Black Panther, under a motto that named the tradition exactly: *solidarity, not charity*. Its clinic and relief operation served everyone the flood had touched, and drew tens of thousands of volunteers from across the country.[^2]

After Hurricane Sandy in 2012, **Occupy Sandy** turned a protest network into a relief infrastructure that at its height moved more volunteers through church-basement hubs than the official agencies could field, and it served the Rockaways and Staten Island block by block, for whoever lived there. Reporters covering FEMA's slow response kept arriving at the same conclusion, which was that where the agency fell short, the volunteers were already there.[^3]

In the spring of 2020, hundreds of COVID mutual aid networks assembled in a matter of weeks, and their organising unit was geography rather than identity, because people organised by the building, the block, and the zip code. Bed-Stuy Strong grew to thousands of members buying groceries for neighbours they had never met, and a survey of pandemic giving found that most of it moved outside formal charity entirely. In January 2025, as wildfires moved through Los Angeles, immigrant organisations and neighbourhood networks ran supply hubs for everyone displaced, including the undocumented residents whom federal disaster relief excludes by rule.[^4]

## Need and Presence

These episodes are systematic rather than exceptional, and there is a scientific literature that says so. Disaster sociology has replicated the same finding for six decades, and the finding is that catastrophe reliably produces new aid groups organised around the shared situation rather than around pre-existing social boundaries. Charles Fritz, the field's founder, called the phenomenon a *community of sufferers*, meaning a new community whose membership is defined by the event and explicitly not by the prior social system. The Disaster Research Center's typology gave the groups a name, *emergent groups*, and half a century of studies confirmed the rule that the greater the disaster, the more such groups emerge. Crisis strips the situation down to the tradition's actual organising principle, and that principle turns out to be need and presence rather than ethnicity.[^5]

One honest limit belongs in this account, and it strengthens the argument rather than weakening it. Everyday mutual aid in the nineteenth century mostly did run within communities, although the boundaries it ran inside were imposed at least as much as they were chosen. If the practice were *inherently* ethnic, however, it would stay ethnic under pressure, and it never has. Every time the boundary between communities is overwhelmed by a shared emergency, the aid crosses it, immediately and in both directions, from the plague year of 1793 to the fires of 2025.[^6]
