On 14 August 1935, Franklin Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act. The standard histories of American welfare begin their story there, because in those accounts the years before the Act held nothing except the poorhouse and private charity, while the years after it saw the beginnings of a real safety net. Told that way, the story is a rescue, in which the state finally arrived in an empty room.[^1]

![Roosevelt signs the Social Security Act, 1935](/images/b1/ssa-signing-1935.jpg "Franklin Roosevelt signs the Social Security Act, 14 August 1935, with Labor Secretary Frances Perkins and the bill's congressional sponsors behind him. © Public domain, Social Security Administration, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Signing_Of_The_Social_Security_Act.jpg")

The room was not empty. When the Act was signed, fraternal societies were still, in David Beito's phrase, the leading providers of social welfare in the United States, and their membership had reached an estimated half of the adult male population at its height. Millions of families still held fraternal life insurance and sick benefits, and in the depths of the Depression itself, before federal relief reached most of them, communities had already organised new provision on a very large scale.[^2]

## What Was Already There

![Breadline in New York City, 1932](/images/b1/breadline-1932.jpg "A breadline in New York City, 1932. Before federal relief arrived, free food in the cities was distributed largely with private and community funds. © Public domain, National Archives, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Depression,_Breadlines-long_line_of_people_waiting_to_be_fed,_New_York_City,_in_the_absence_of_substantial_government..._-_NARA_-_196506.tif")

In the early 1930s, unemployed workers across the country built self-help cooperatives. Some of these were labour exchanges, in which members traded hours of work with one another, and others were cooperative farms and canneries, or barter networks in which the jobless traded work for food and fuel. California alone counted scores of them, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics thought the movement significant enough to survey it, publishing an accounting of the nation's cooperative productive enterprises in 1933. Contemporary observers debated in the social work journals whether "self-help" was the future of relief.[^3]

Black communities were largely excluded from local relief administration, and they would later be excluded from key provisions of the New Deal itself, so they ran a parallel cooperative surge through the 1930s and 1940s. They organised buying clubs, credit unions, and cooperative stores, and many of these were built through churches and mutual aid societies that had been operating since the previous century.[^4]

None of this could have carried the Depression alone, because the scale of the collapse overwhelmed every institution in the country, and the fraternal funds were no exception. The lodges could not have substituted for Social Security. But the New Deal did not fill a vacuum. It entered a field crowded with functioning mutual institutions, and over the following decades it displaced them.

## The Quiet Displacement

After 1935, fraternal membership fell and kept falling. The societies' core products, which were the sick benefit, the old-age fund, and the widow's payment, were now offered by the state, and they were funded by payroll taxes that members had to pay whether they kept their lodge membership or not. Commercial insurers, who had spent decades lobbying state legislatures to restrict fraternal insurance, took much of what remained. The lodge doctor, who was already under organised attack from the medical profession, disappeared within a generation. The decline had several causes working together, and historians still argue over their relative weights, because the Depression had damaged the benefit funds, some orders had genuine actuarial weaknesses, and tastes among the young were changing. But the timing is not subtle. The societies' welfare functions went first, and the social functions built around them followed.[^5]

## Written Out

The displacement itself was never really recorded as one. The standard surveys of American welfare history run from the poor law to the welfare state, and community provision appears only as a curiosity along the way. Andrew Morris, writing in a Cambridge history of the voluntary sector, notes that Michael Katz's leading synthesis of the field devotes three pages to voluntary agencies after the Second World War. A century and a half of self-organised provision, which at its peak had enrolled a third or more of the country's men, contracted into a footnote. The institutions were displaced first, and the memory of them was displaced afterwards.[^6]

That erasure shaped how every later revival of the practice would be understood. Because the tradition had become invisible, each new outbreak of mutual aid, in the civil rights movement, in the Panthers' breakfast programmes, in the AIDS crisis, and in the pandemic, would be narrated as a novelty or an aberration rather than as the continuation of a practice older than the Republic. The decades in which the textbooks say the tradition no longer existed are, in fact, the decades in which it did some of its most consequential work.
