In the spring of 1787, two founding meetings took place in Philadelphia within a few blocks of each other. The more famous of the two ran from May to September, when fifty-five delegates met in the Pennsylvania State House, later renamed Independence Hall, to draft the Constitution of the United States and to settle who would be recognised as a citizen of the new Republic. The other took place on 12 April, when a group of free Black men gathered to found the **Free African Society**, an organisation formed to provide for the people whom those rules would exclude.[^1]

![Back of the State House, Philadelphia, 1799](/images/a1/state-house-birch-1800.jpg "The Pennsylvania State House, now Independence Hall, where the Constitutional Convention met from May to September 1787. Drawn and engraved by W. Birch and Son, 1799. © Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Back_of_the_State_House_Birch%27s_Views_Plate_22.jpg")

Its founders, **Richard Allen** and **Absalom Jones**, had been born enslaved, had purchased their own freedom, and had become religious leaders in Philadelphia's free Black community. Because their society was founded in the same city and the same year as the Constitutional Convention, the two institutions can be dated against each other with unusual precision, and that precision matters for how the history is understood. Organised mutual aid in the United States was not a later improvisation, invented to fill gaps that appeared as the Republic developed. It began when the Republic began, and it was built by people whom the founding documents did not count as members.

## How One Shilling a Month Protected a Family

The society's founding charter, its Preamble and Articles of Association, set out the arrangement in precise terms. Each member paid one shilling in silver Pennsylvania currency into a common fund every month. When a member fell sick and could not work, the fund supported him until he recovered. When a member died, the society paid for his burial, and it continued to support his widow and his fatherless children afterwards.[^2] Members paid in while they were well and drew out when they were not, and since every member was bound by the same obligations, the arrangement was reciprocal rather than charitable. No benefactor stood above the members, and no member had to prove that he deserved help, for the right to draw on the fund had already been purchased by the same dues that everyone paid.

The promise of burial deserves particular explanation, because in eighteenth-century Philadelphia it was not a ceremonial detail. The graves of Black Philadelphians were routinely robbed to supply cadavers for medical dissection, and a society that guaranteed its members a protected burial was therefore providing a form of security that the city itself did not offer.[^3]

![Preamble of the Free African Society](/images/a1/fas-preamble-1787.jpg "The Preamble of the Free African Society, as printed in William Douglass, Annals of the First African Church, 1862: a society formed 'to support one another in sickness, and for the benefit of their widows and fatherless children.' © Public domain, via Internet Archive https://archive.org/details/annalsoffirstafr00doug")

Taken together, these functions amount to the work of a civic institution. The society insured its members against sickness, cared for them when they fell ill, supported their surviving dependants, and buried them with dignity, and it did all of this decades before any government or commercial insurer offered such protections to this community. The members also governed the institution themselves, under rules that they had written and could amend.

## The Fever Year of 1793

Six years after its founding, the society was tested by one of the worst epidemics in American history. When yellow fever swept Philadelphia in 1793, it killed roughly one in ten residents, and most people with the means to leave the city did so. The members of the Free African Society stayed. They nursed the sick, dug graves, buried the dead, and carried the ill to quarantine quarters outside the city, and they did this work for Black and white Philadelphians alike, at a moment when the prevailing medical theory held, mistakenly, that Black residents were immune to the disease.[^4]

When a prominent publisher afterwards accused the city's Black nurses of having profiteered from the epidemic, Jones and Allen answered him in print. Their pamphlet, *A Narrative of the Proceedings of the Black People, During the Late Awful Calamity in Philadelphia*, defended the community's conduct in careful documentary detail. To protect the pamphlet, they registered it for federal copyright under the authority of Article One, Section 8 of the Constitution, and their registration was the first federal copyright ever obtained by African Americans. In other words, when the society's record was publicly attacked, its founders defended that record by using the legal machinery of the document that had been drafted a few blocks from their founding meeting.[^5]

## A Hundred Societies Follow the Design

The Philadelphia society proved to be a prototype. By 1838, more than a hundred mutual aid societies built on the same pattern had been established across the country.[^6] The design repeated because the circumstances that produced it repeated. Free Black communities in other cities were excluded from formal citizenship in the same ways, and they therefore faced the same needs for insurance, care, and burial. Because the model of regular dues, defined benefits, and collective governance was simple, it could be rebuilt wherever those needs existed.

The Free African Society was not the first organisation of its kind, and the tradition to which it belongs is older than its own founding date. The Free African Union Society of Newport, Rhode Island had organised on similar terms in 1780, and reciprocal care among Indigenous nations and enslaved communities is older than the colonies themselves, although it rarely entered the written record in the form of charters and dues books.[^7] The Philadelphia society carries particular documentary weight, however, since its charter survives, its founders left first-person accounts, and its founding can be dated precisely against the Constitutional Convention meeting in the same city.

A society of a few dozen members in one city was a modest beginning, but the gathering of fifty-five delegates a few blocks away was also small, and the significance of both meetings lies in what grew from them. Over the nineteenth century, communities excluded from formal citizenship built civic infrastructure across the country on the Philadelphia pattern, and the tradition continued to grow until, by 1920, it encompassed one in three adult American men and reached further still through their families.
