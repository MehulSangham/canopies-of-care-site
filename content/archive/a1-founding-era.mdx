In the spring of 1787, Philadelphia hosted two founding acts. From May to September, fifty-five delegates met in Independence Hall to draft the rules for who would be recognised as American. A few blocks away, on April 12 of the same year, a group of free Black men gathered in an unnamed room to found the **Free African Society**, an organisation built to provide for the people those rules would exclude.[^1]

![Back of the State House, Philadelphia, 1799](/images/a1/state-house-birch-1800.jpg "The Pennsylvania State House, now Independence Hall, where the Constitutional Convention met from May to September 1787. Drawn and engraved by W. Birch and Son, 1799. © Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Back_of_the_State_House_Birch%27s_Views_Plate_22.jpg")

Its founders, **Richard Allen** and **Absalom Jones**, had been born enslaved, had purchased their own freedom, and had become religious leaders in Philadelphia's free Black community. The society they built alongside the Constitution, in the same city and the same year, places organised mutual aid at the very beginning of the Republic. Care has been part of the American founding from the start, built by the people the founding documents excluded.

## One Shilling in Silver

The society operated on reciprocal obligation. Its Preamble and Articles of Association required each member to contribute one shilling in silver Pennsylvania currency every month, and in exchange the society provided support during sickness, burial services, and provision for widows and fatherless children.[^2] Members paid in and drew out according to need, on terms the founding charter specified precisely. Even the dead were cared for: burial provision carried real weight in a city where the graves of Black Philadelphians were routinely robbed for medical dissection.[^3]

![Preamble of the Free African Society](/images/a1/fas-preamble-1787.jpg "The Preamble of the Free African Society, as printed in William Douglass, Annals of the First African Church, 1862: a society formed 'to support one another in sickness, and for the benefit of their widows and fatherless children.' © Public domain, via Internet Archive https://archive.org/details/annalsoffirstafr00doug")

These were the functions of a civic institution: insurance, healthcare, social security, and dignified burial, organised decades before any state or market would offer them to this community, and governed by the members themselves.

## The Fever Year

The society's early record shows what that infrastructure could do under pressure. When yellow fever swept Philadelphia in 1793, killing roughly one in ten residents and emptying the city of anyone wealthy enough to flee, members of the Free African Society stayed. They nursed the sick, dug graves, buried the dead, and carried the ill to quarantine quarters outside the city, serving Black and white Philadelphians alike at a moment when the prevailing medical theory held, mistakenly, that Black residents were immune.[^4]

When a prominent publisher afterwards accused the city's Black nurses of profiteering, Jones and Allen answered in print. Their pamphlet, *A Narrative of the Proceedings of the Black People, During the Late Awful Calamity in Philadelphia*, defended the community's conduct in careful documentary detail, and to protect it they secured the first federal copyright ever obtained by African Americans, citing the authority of Article One, Section 8 of the very Constitution drafted down the street from their founding meeting.[^5]

## A Hundred Societies

The Philadelphia society proved to be a prototype. By 1838, more than a hundred mutual aid societies built on the same pattern had been established across the country.[^6] The design travelled because the need was structural: exclusion from formal citizenship created a permanent demand for community-built care, and the society's model of regular dues, defined benefits, and collective governance proved repeatable wherever that demand existed.

Older traditions ran beneath it. The Free African Union Society of Newport, Rhode Island had organised on similar terms in 1780, and reciprocal care among Indigenous nations and enslaved communities is older than the colonies themselves, though it rarely entered the written record in the form of charters and dues books.[^7] The Philadelphia society carries the documentary weight of this history because its charter survives, its founders left first-person accounts, and its founding sits beside the Constitutional Convention with dated certainty.

A society of a few dozen members in one city was a modest beginning, and so was the gathering of fifty-five delegates a few blocks away. Traditions begin somewhere. From this seed, excluded communities across the nineteenth century built parallel civic infrastructure on the same pattern, and the tradition grew until, by 1920, it encompassed one in three adult American men and reached further still through their families. Philadelphia held two founding meetings in 1787. This archive is the record of the second.
