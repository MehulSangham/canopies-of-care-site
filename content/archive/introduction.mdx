In 1787, two things happened in Philadelphia. From May to September, the Constitutional Convention drafted the rules for who would be recognised as American, and excluded most of the people living in the country from that recognition. In April of the same year, in the same city, two free Black ministers named Richard Allen and Absalom Jones founded the Free African Society, the first formally organised mutual aid network in the United States. It provided burial, sickness benefits, and support for widows and orphans to people the Constitution refused to see.[^1]

Both are founding acts. Both began traditions that run unbroken to the present. Only one of them is remembered as the founding, and this archive is the record of the other one.

## The Argument

Mutual aid, meaning communities organising collective care on terms of reciprocity, is a founding American tradition, as old as the Republic itself. Through it, excluded communities built the actual infrastructure of civic life: insurance, healthcare, credit, schools, burial, and relief, organised by the people who needed them, for each other, decades and sometimes centuries before any state or market would provide them. From the Free African Society in 1787 through the immigrant benevolent societies, the fraternal lodges, the Depression-era cooperatives, the care systems of the civil rights movement, the Black Panther survival programmes, the AIDS-era buddy networks, and the disaster relief hubs of the present, Americans have organised collective care every time law and markets refused them, in every generation, without exception.

The tradition's absence from the national story is structural rather than accidental. It was displaced by law, absorbed by the state without credit, kept out of the archives, and outbuilt by a competing account of American identity, one of bounded citizenship and individual self-reliance, whose institutions were funded and constructed deliberately. Recovering the record changes what "American" means, which is why the record has been worth suppressing and is now worth assembling.

## The Shape of It

The archive makes its case in four sections, and each one answers the strongest objection to the section before it.

**Section A, The Founding Tradition,** establishes that the tradition is real, old, and large: founded alongside the Constitution, rebuilt independently by every excluded community in the country, and grown by 1920 to enrol one in three adult American men.

**Section B, Through the Evolution of American Identity,** answers the objection that this is distant history. It follows the tradition through the New Deal, the civil rights movement, the Panther programmes, the AIDS crisis, and the maternal care networks that have run continuously since before the Republic.

**Section C, The Transcendence Across Identities,** answers the hardest objection: that mutual aid was only ever each group caring for its own. In crisis, in labour, and by deliberate design, the aid crossed every line, and where it stayed bounded, the boundary was drawn by exclusion imposed from outside.

**Section D, The Contest Over American Identity,** answers the question the first three sections make unavoidable: how a tradition this large disappeared from the story. It documents the legal machinery that burdens the practice, the institutional machinery built to circulate a rival account of the country, and the plan currently translating that account into government.

## What This Archive Is Not

It does not claim that mutual aid is the only American tradition. The exclusionary tradition is also real, also documented, also continuous. The claim is equal standing: the mutual aid tradition is as real, as old, and as continuous as the account that displaced it, and any honest telling of American identity must include both.

It is not an organising manual. Others have written well about how to practise mutual aid now; this archive tells the history of the people who have always practised it.[^2]

It is not a policy proposal. It is the knowledge base on which arguments, curricula, and policy can later stand: a citable account of a tradition that has never had one.

## How to Read It

The pages run in order, A1 to D4, and each one hands off to the next, so the archive reads as a single story. Each page also stands alone, resting on named, checkable sources; the footnotes carry both the notes and the full citations, because a contestation document that cannot survive hostile checking is no use to anyone.
