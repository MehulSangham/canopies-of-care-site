Long before any charter was signed in Philadelphia, the nations of this continent ran complete economies of mutual obligation. These systems fed the hungry, insured the unlucky against misfortune, redistributed wealth, and kept their own records, and they did all of this without a state and without a market in the European sense. The tradition they carried is not a chapter of the American civic story. It precedes the Republic, it was outlawed by the Republic's government in documents that survive, and it continues today as the practice of sovereign nations. The mutual aid societies that free Black communities began founding in 1787 therefore did not appear on an empty continent. An older tradition of organised reciprocity was already here, and it was already complete.[^1]

## The Potlatch, an Economy of Obligation

On the Northwest Coast, from the Tlingit villages of southeast Alaska down through the Kwakwaka'wakw and Coast Salish territories, the central economic institution was the **potlatch**, a great feast at which a host validated a name, a marriage, an inheritance, or a territorial claim by giving wealth away in front of witnesses. Blankets, canoes, food, and coppers moved outward from the host to every guest, in proportion to each guest's standing. The word itself comes from the Chinook trade jargon for *to give*.[^2]

The potlatch was an economic system as much as a ceremony. A family that had accumulated wealth did not store it, because in this economy wealth conferred standing only when it was given away, and a household's position rose with what it distributed rather than with what it kept. Each guest who accepted a gift took on an obligation to give in return at a feast of his own, and over the years these exchanges built up a standing network of obligations that ran through the whole community. A household whose fishing failed or whose house burned could therefore call on the claims that its own past giving had created, which meant that the feast system worked as insurance as well as redistribution. The potlatch also served as the community's public record. The guests were witnesses to every name, marriage, inheritance, and territorial claim that the feast confirmed, and some guests were paid specifically to remember what had been transacted. On the Plains, the **giveaway** honoured relatives and marked the great occasions of a life on the same principle of distribution. Among the Diné, the principle had a name, **k'é**, which describes the web of kinship obligation binding a person to family, clan, community, and land, and which makes care a duty owed within a relationship rather than a feeling about it.[^3]

## The Choctaw Gift to Ireland, 1847

In the spring of 1847, that ethic reached across an ocean. On 23 March, at Skullyville in Indian Territory, the United States agent read aloud an appeal for the starving poor of Ireland, and the Choctaw gathered there took up a collection. Contemporary accounts differ on the sum. The printed acknowledgment records $170 from "the children of the forest, our red brethren of the Choctaw Nation," while a newspaper report of the meeting records $710 collected from everyone present, agents, missionaries, traders, and Indians, with "a considerable portion" given by the Choctaw themselves. No account disputes the circumstances in which the money was given. Sixteen years earlier, the Choctaw had been marched off their homeland east of the Mississippi on the road they remember as the Trail of Tears, and thousands of them had died walking it. A nation that had just survived dispossession and famine collected money for strangers who were starving under a different empire, because it recognised what was happening to them.[^4]

![Kindred Spirits, Midleton, County Cork](/images/a0/kindred-spirits-midleton.jpg "Kindred Spirits by Alex Pentek, Bailick Park, Midleton, County Cork: nine steel eagle feathers forming an empty bowl, dedicated in 2017 by Choctaw Chief Gary Batton in commemoration of the 1847 gift. © Photograph by Maoileann, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sculpture-1014998,_Midleton,_Co._Cork,_Ireland.jpg")

Ireland did not forget. In 2017 the town of Midleton, County Cork, dedicated *Kindred Spirits*, a sculpture of nine steel eagle feathers twenty feet high, arranged in the shape of an empty bowl, and a delegation from the Choctaw Nation led by Chief Gary Batton travelled to Ireland for the ceremony. One hundred and seventy years after the collection at Skullyville, a town on the far side of the Atlantic was still keeping the memory of the gift, which is the way a gift economy has always kept its records.[^5]

## The Law Against Giving, 1883

What the United States government did with this tradition is preserved in its own files. In December 1882, Secretary of the Interior Henry Teller instructed the Commissioner of Indian Affairs to end "the old heathenish dances" and feasts, and on 30 March 1883 Commissioner Hiram Price issued the **Rules Governing the Court of Indian Offenses**. The fourth rule made the sun dance, the scalp dance, the war dance, "and all other so-called feasts assimilating thereto" punishable offences, and the penalties were withheld rations and imprisonment in the agency jail. Other rules criminalised the medicine men and the distribution of a dead person's property at funerals. From 1883, therefore, the feasts at which wealth changed hands, the ceremonies through which the community insured its members, and the rites by which property returned to circulation at death were all offences against the United States, triable at every agency. Canada reached the same target even more directly, because its Indian Act amendment of 1885 outlawed the potlatch by name, and the ban stayed in force until 1951.[^6]

![Dancers at the Klukwan potlatch, 1898](/images/a0/klukwan-dancers-1898.jpg "Dancers at the Klukwan potlatch, 14 October 1898, fifteen years after the Code of Indian Offenses made such feasts punishable. The practice continued, watched, renamed, and moved out of sight when it had to be. Photograph by Arthur C. Pillsbury. © Public domain, via the Digital Public Library of America https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dancers_at_Klukwan_Potlatch_ceremony,_Alaska,_October_14,_1898_-_DPLA_-_773ec06a7367036ee04c7a1f9175c35c.jpg")

The reasoning behind the law was stated just as plainly, by the senator whose name is on the companion policy. Henry Dawes visited the Cherokee Nation and described what he had found there to the Lake Mohonk Conference in 1885. He reported that there was not a family in the nation without a home and not a pauper in the nation, that the nation did not owe a dollar, and that it had built its own capitol, its own schools, and its own hospitals. Then he named what he considered the defect. "They have got as far as they can go, because they own their land in common... There is no selfishness, which is at the bottom of civilization. Till this people will consent to give up their lands, and divide them among their citizens so that each can own the land he cultivates, they will not make much more progress." Two years later, the **General Allotment Act**, known as the Dawes Act, began breaking communal territory into individually owned parcels, and by the time allotment was halted in 1934, tribal landholdings had fallen from 138 million acres to 48 million. A senator had surveyed a nation with no poverty and no debt, had diagnosed the absence of selfishness as its disease, and had written the cure into law.[^7]

Dawes's speech is the oldest and plainest surviving statement of the belief against which the mutual aid tradition has always been measured. Where the potlatch and the giveaway treated belonging as something a person practises by providing for the people around them, Dawes argued that civilisation begins with what a person keeps. In 1885 that belief was not implied or coded. It was entered into the conference record by its author, and the practice it condemned was not a loose analogy to mutual aid. The feasts, the giveaways, and the commonly held lands were mutual aid, and they were outlawed by name and by function.

The bans did not end the practice. Potlatches continued at villages like Klukwan in full view of visiting photographers, moved indoors and out of season when government agents pressed, and were folded into winter gatherings and Christmas exchanges when they had to be. The federal dance bans were formally lifted in 1934, and the underlying right to practise was not affirmed in federal law until 1978. By then the communities had simply outwaited the statute.[^8]

## The Gift Returns, 2020

The tradition is not historical, and its most recent demonstration is very recent indeed. In March 2020, as COVID-19 moved through the Navajo Nation faster than almost anywhere else in the country, a former attorney general of the Navajo Nation named **Ethel Branch** opened a small online fundraiser to buy food and water for elders, and she hoped it might raise fifty thousand dollars. Within weeks, the **Navajo & Hopi Families COVID-19 Relief Fund** had grown into a full relief operation, run by a team of Navajo and Hopi women with hundreds of volunteers and dozens of local chapters, and it delivered food, water, and supplies across a reservation the size of West Virginia. One of its leads, Cassandra Begay, described the fund's operating principle to the Navajo Times in the word that names the old ethic: "We're coming from a place of compassion, strength and love for our community, which has been fundamentally instilled in us in terms of k'é." The fund raised eighteen million dollars in its first year.[^9]

![Navajo children with their family's pick-up truck, 1972](/images/a0/navajo-children-1972.jpg "Navajo children with their family's pick-up truck, Navajo Nation, 1972. Photograph by Terry Eiler for DOCUMERICA. On a reservation where a third of households hauled their own water, the networks of 2020 ran on exactly these family lines. © Public domain, National Archives, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:NAVAJO_CHILDREN_EXAMINE_THEIR_FAMILY_PICK-UP_TRUCK_-_NARA_-_544402.jpg")

In May 2020, the fund's donation page began filling with Irish names, with Siobháns and Pádraigs, O'Learys and Gallaghers. Within days, roughly twenty thousand donors from Ireland had given about six hundred and seventy thousand dollars, and many of them explained their reason in the donation messages. They remembered the Choctaw. "In our darkest hour, your ancestors taught us the meaning of unadulterated altruism," one Irish donor wrote. "173 years later, it's not forgotten." Others posted an Irish proverb: *Ar scáth a chéile a mhaireann na daoine*. In each other's shadows the people live.[^10]

A gift given by a dispossessed nation in 1847 had been answered, across an ocean and seven generations, into the hands of a different nation practising the same ethic under a different name. No institution had kept the account open during those one hundred and seventy-three years. The memory was carried by the people themselves, in Ireland and in the Choctaw Nation, which is how a gift economy has always kept its records. The government that outlawed the giveaway in 1883 assumed that what is given away is gone. The evidence shows the opposite, because what was given away was remembered for seven generations, and then it came back.[^11]
