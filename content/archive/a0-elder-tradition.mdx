This archive begins in 1787, with a founding that ran parallel to the Constitution. This page stands before that date, deliberately, because the practice the archive documents has an elder. Long before any charter was signed in Philadelphia, the nations of this continent ran complete economies of mutual obligation: systems that fed the hungry, insured the unlucky, redistributed wealth, and kept their own records, all without a state and without a market in the European sense. That tradition is not a chapter of the American civic story. It precedes the Republic, it was outlawed by the Republic's government in documents that survive, and it continues today as the practice of sovereign nations. It belongs at the head of this record the way an elder belongs at the head of a table.[^1]

## An Economy of Obligation

On the Northwest Coast, from the Tlingit villages of southeast Alaska down through the Kwakwaka'wakw and Coast Salish territories, the central economic institution was the **potlatch**: a great feast at which a host validated a name, a marriage, an inheritance, or a claim by giving wealth away in front of witnesses. Blankets, canoes, food, and coppers moved outward from the host to every guest, in proportion to standing. The word itself comes from the Chinook trade jargon for *to give*.[^2]

A single potlatch could draw hundreds of guests from villages days of travel apart. Photograph by Arthur C. Pillsbury. © Public domain, via Seattle Public Library and the Digital Public Library of America https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chilkat_Indians_watching_Klukwan_Potlatch_ceremony,_Alaska,_October_11,_1898_-_DPLA_-_0921f16ef0ee17fffba4a76e25103b2d.jpg")

Read as economics, the potlatch did the work that dues books and benefit schedules would do for the mutual aid societies in the pages that follow. Wealth that accumulated in one house was sent around the community on a fixed loom of obligation, and every gift created a claim that would return when the giver's own luck turned. Read as law, it did even more: the guests were witnesses, and some were paid specifically to remember what had been transacted, so that the feast itself was the community's public record of names, territories, marriages, and inheritances. Standing was measured by what a person circulated, not what they kept. On the Plains, the **giveaway** honoured relatives and marked the great occasions of a life on the same principle, and among the Diné the principle had a name that contains the whole system: **k'é**, the kinship obligation that relates a person to family, clan, community, and land, and makes care the substance of the relation rather than a sentiment about it.[^3]

## The Gift That Crossed an Ocean

The reach of that ethic is on record in one of the most remarkable documents of the 1840s. On 23 March 1847, at Skullyville in Indian Territory, the United States agent read aloud an appeal for the starving poor of Ireland, and the Choctaw gathered there took up a collection. Contemporary accounts differ on the sum: the printed acknowledgment records $170 from "the children of the forest, our red brethren of the Choctaw Nation," while a newspaper report of the meeting records $710 collected from everyone present, agents, missionaries, traders, and Indians, "a considerable portion" given by the Choctaw themselves. What no account disputes is the circumstance. Sixteen years earlier the Choctaw had been marched off their homeland east of the Mississippi on the road they remember as the Trail of Tears, and thousands had died walking it. A nation that had just survived dispossession and famine collected money for strangers starving under a different empire, because it recognised them.[^4]

![Kindred Spirits, Midleton, County Cork](/images/a0/kindred-spirits-midleton.jpg "Kindred Spirits by Alex Pentek, Bailick Park, Midleton, County Cork: nine steel eagle feathers forming an empty bowl, dedicated in 2017 by Choctaw Chief Gary Batton in commemoration of the 1847 gift. © Photograph by Maoileann, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sculpture-1014998,_Midleton,_Co._Cork,_Ireland.jpg")

Ireland did not forget. In 2017 the town of Midleton, County Cork, dedicated *Kindred Spirits*, nine steel eagle feathers twenty feet high, arranged as a bowl holding nothing but air: the gift of food, remembered as a shape. The sculpture matters to this page less as a monument than as a receipt. The gift economy keeps its records in witnesses, and here was a town in Ireland testifying, one hundred and seventy years later, that the obligation was still live.[^5]

## A Law Against Giving

What the United States government did with this tradition is preserved in its own files. In December 1882, Secretary of the Interior Henry Teller instructed the Commissioner of Indian Affairs to end "the old heathenish dances" and feasts, and on 30 March 1883 Commissioner Hiram Price issued the **Rules Governing the Court of Indian Offenses**. The fourth rule made the sun dance, the scalp dance, the war dance, "and all other so-called feasts assimilating thereto" punishable offences, with penalties of withheld rations and imprisonment in the agency jail. Other rules criminalised the medicine men and the funerary distribution of a dead person's property. The feasts at which wealth changed hands, the ceremonies through which the community insured its members, and the rites by which property returned to circulation at death were, from 1883, offences against the United States, triable at every agency. Canada had reached the same target more directly: its Indian Act amendment of 1885 outlawed the potlatch by name, and kept it outlawed until 1951.[^6]

![Dancers at the Klukwan potlatch, 1898](/images/a0/klukwan-dancers-1898.jpg "Dancers at the Klukwan potlatch, 14 October 1898, fifteen years after the Code of Indian Offenses made such feasts punishable. The practice continued, watched, renamed, and moved out of sight when it had to be. Photograph by Arthur C. Pillsbury. © Public domain, via the Digital Public Library of America https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dancers_at_Klukwan_Potlatch_ceremony,_Alaska,_October_14,_1898_-_DPLA_-_773ec06a7367036ee04c7a1f9175c35c.jpg")

The reasoning behind the law was stated just as plainly, by the senator whose name is on the companion policy. Henry Dawes visited the Cherokee Nation and reported what he found to the Lake Mohonk Conference in 1885: not a family without a home, not a pauper in the nation, not a dollar of debt, a capitol, schools, and hospitals built by the nation itself. Then he named the defect. "They have got as far as they can go, because they own their land in common... There is no selfishness, which is at the bottom of civilization. Till this people will consent to give up their lands, and divide them among their citizens so that each can own the land he cultivates, they will not make much more progress." Two years later the **General Allotment Act**, the Dawes Act, began breaking communal territory into individual parcels, and by the time allotment was halted in 1934, tribal landholdings had fallen from 138 million acres to 48 million. A senator surveyed a nation with no poverty and no debt, diagnosed the absence of selfishness as the disease, and wrote the cure into law.[^7]

This is the oldest and plainest statement of the rival account that runs through the whole archive. Where the practice says belonging is what you do for the people around you, the rival account says civilisation begins with what you keep. In 1885 that account was not implied, not coded, and not deniable. It was entered into the conference record by its author, and the practice it was aimed at was not a metaphor for mutual aid. It was mutual aid, outlawed by name and by function.

The bans did not work. Potlatches continued at villages like Klukwan under the eye of the cameras, moved indoors and out of season when agents pressed, and were folded into winter gatherings and Christmas exchanges when they had to be. The dance bans were formally lifted in 1934, and the underlying claim of religious freedom was not vindicated in federal law until 1978. The practice had simply outwaited the statute, which is what this archive will show the practice doing, in other communities, for the next hundred and forty years.[^8]

## The Gift Returns

The tradition's present tense arrived with the pandemic. In March 2020, as COVID-19 moved through the Navajo Nation faster than almost anywhere in the country, a former Navajo Nation attorney general named **Ethel Branch** opened a small online fundraiser to buy food and water for elders. She hoped for fifty thousand dollars. Within weeks the **Navajo & Hopi Families COVID-19 Relief Fund** was a full relief infrastructure, run by a team of Navajo and Hopi women, hundreds of volunteers, and dozens of local chapters, delivering food, water, and supplies across a reservation the size of West Virginia. Its organisers described the operating principle in one word. "We're coming from a place of compassion, strength and love for our community, which has been fundamentally instilled in us in terms of k'é," one of its leads, Cassandra Begay, told the Navajo Times. The fund raised eighteen million dollars in its first year.[^9]

![Navajo children with their family's pick-up truck, 1972](/images/a0/navajo-children-1972.jpg "Navajo children with their family's pick-up truck, Navajo Nation, 1972. Photograph by Terry Eiler for DOCUMERICA. On a reservation where a third of households hauled their own water, the networks of 2020 ran on exactly these family lines. © Public domain, National Archives, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:NAVAJO_CHILDREN_EXAMINE_THEIR_FAMILY_PICK-UP_TRUCK_-_NARA_-_544402.jpg")

Then the witnesses spoke up. In May 2020 the fund's donation page began filling with Irish names, Siobhán and Pádraig and O'Leary and Gallagher, twenty thousand of them within days, six hundred and seventy thousand dollars, many carrying the same message: we remember the Choctaw. "In our darkest hour, your ancestors taught us the meaning of unadulterated altruism," one Irish donor wrote. "173 years later, it's not forgotten." Others posted an Irish proverb that could stand as the epigraph for every page in this archive: *Ar scáth a chéile a mhaireann na daoine*. In each other's shadows the people live.[^10]

The circle that closed in those comment threads had been open for one hundred and seventy-three years, and it makes the argument of this page better than any summary could. A gift given by a dispossessed nation in 1847 was answered, across an ocean and seven generations, into the hands of a different nation practising the same ethic under a different name. The record was kept the whole time, by witnesses, which is how the elder tradition has always kept its records. The government that outlawed the giveaway assumed that what is given away is gone. The evidence says otherwise: what is given away is remembered, and it comes back.[^11]
