In November 2014, police in Fort Lauderdale, Florida, stopped a ninety-year-old Second World War veteran named Arnold Abbott as he served hot meals to homeless people in a public park, and charged him under a city ordinance restricting outdoor food sharing. He had been doing this weekly for twenty-three years. He was charged twice more within the month, each time with a possible sixty days in jail, and each time he went back out with the trays.[^1]

Abbott's arrest belonged to a national pattern. A survey that year counted twenty-one cities that had restricted sharing food with homeless people since the start of 2013, with more ordinances pending. The arrests have continued across a decade and a map: Food Not Bombs volunteers charged in Houston, West Palm Beach in 2023, Kansas City in 2024, Tulsa in 2026. The courts keep siding with the volunteers (in 2018 a federal appeals court held that sharing food in public is expressive conduct protected by the First Amendment), and the cities keep passing ordinances anyway. The record shows persistent attempts at criminalisation that continue despite judicial rebukes, which is in some ways more revealing than success would be.[^2]

## Taxed, Unrecognised, Excluded

The quieter mechanisms sit in the tax code. American law defines a tax-free gift by a 1960 Supreme Court standard, "detached and disinterested generosity," a definition built for donations to strangers through registered charities. Mutual aid is neither detached nor disinterested; it is reciprocal by design, and the code has no category for it. Neighbours who pool money through payment apps can find the total reported to the IRS as business income on a 1099-K form. In 2021 Congress dropped the reporting threshold from 20,000 dollars to 600 dollars, sweeping thousands of informal aid networks into tax paperwork; a 2025 law restored the higher threshold, in what the researchers who study the problem call a welcome but fragile reprieve. Meanwhile the majority of pandemic-era giving, 68 per cent by Indiana University's measure, moved outside the registered-charity system entirely, which means the dominant form of American generosity now operates in a legal architecture that does not recognise it.[^3]

Eligibility rules draw the same boundary from the other side. Federal disaster assistance requires citizenship or qualifying immigration status, a rule grounded in the 1996 welfare reform's declaration of immigrant self-reliance. In 2025 the exclusion widened: billions in disaster aid were held for immigration-enforcement review, and a Department of Homeland Security directive moved to bar organisations receiving federal disaster funds from serving undocumented residents. Every such rule makes the informal networks the only remaining provision for the excluded, while the same legal order declines to recognise, fund, or in some cities even permit those networks.[^4]

## The Fourth Mechanism

The last mechanism is the one this archive has already documented twice at full length: absorption. The fraternal benefit systems were displaced by commercial insurance and Social Security, and vanished from the story of the safety net. The Panther breakfast programme was federalised while the FBI dismantled the organisation that invented it. In both cases the state gained the function and the tradition lost the credit. Intent is debatable in the first case and documented in the second; the structural effect was identical.[^5]

Set the four mechanisms side by side: taxation without recognition, attempted criminalisation, exclusion by eligibility, and absorption. Seen together, the invisibility this archive keeps encountering stops looking like forgetfulness. A practice this old and this large does not fall out of a nation's story by accident. But law and administration are only half the explanation. The other half is a story that was deliberately built to stand where this one should be, and it is the subject of the next page.
