In May 1913, four thousand longshoremen walked off the docks of Philadelphia, and out of the strike came **Local 8** of the Industrial Workers of the World: roughly a third Black, a third Irish and Irish American, a third immigrant Eastern European. Its most prominent leader, Ben Fletcher, was a Black Philadelphian and one of the great labour organisers of his generation. The local rotated its chairmanship between Black and white members, integrated its work gangs on docks that had been strictly segregated, and controlled Philadelphia's waterfront for the better part of a decade. It happened in the same city where the Free African Society had opened the tradition 126 years earlier.[^1]

Local 8 matters to this archive because unions were mutual aid institutions before they were bargaining agents. A union local of this era ran strike funds, sickness and death benefits, relief for members' families, and a hall that functioned the way lodge halls and church basements functioned everywhere else in this history. When the IWW organised across race, it was not only sharing a picket line; it was pooling the machinery of survival. The Wobblies' premise, *one big union*, was a claim about exactly the question this section examines: whether the boundary of solidarity is the ethnic group or the shared condition.[^2]

## The Shared Condition

The 1930s and 1940s tested the premise at industrial scale. CIO unions organised Black and white workers together in Southern tobacco plants, Memphis factories, and Midwestern packinghouses, and historians of the period describe what they built as *civil rights unionism*: benefit systems, grievance protection, and political power, organised across race a generation before the civil rights movement's famous years. Sociologists studying the Depression-era locals found the same mechanism the disaster researchers keep finding in crisis: when the organising basis is the shared situation, the aid follows the situation rather than the bloodline.[^3]

The cooperative movement ran the same experiment in economics. Workers' cooperatives, buying clubs, and farmer alliances repeatedly organised across ethnic lines, and were met, where they threatened the racial order, with the same repression the lodges and the Panthers met. The cotton pickers' strike organised by the Colored Farmers' Alliance in 1891 was crushed by violence; the co-operative tradition it belonged to survived anyway, on both sides of the colour line.[^4]

## The Honest Ledger

None of this was a paradise, and the archive gains nothing from pretending otherwise. The AFL's craft unions excluded Black workers for decades, and much of organised labour enforced the colour line rather than crossing it. But read carefully, the exclusion is evidence for the same conclusion. Where unions chose exclusion, mutual aid stayed bounded, and Black workers built their own parallel institutions, as the immigrant communities of the last century's turn had done. Where unions chose solidarity, in Local 8, in the CIO drives, in the co-ops, mutual aid crossed the line immediately. The boundary tracked the choice, never the practice. Mutual aid follows whatever definition of "us" its organisers adopt, which is precisely why the definition has always been the contested ground. The next page turns to the organisers who chose the widest definition available: everyone in the ward.[^5]
