When the Montgomery bus boycott began in December 1955, a cook named Georgia Gilmore started selling fried chicken sandwiches, pound cake, and full dinners at the mass meetings, and turning the proceeds over to fund the boycott. She organised other cooks into a club whose earnings could not be traced to any member, so that no one could be fired for feeding the movement. Asked where the money came from, she said it came from nowhere, and the Club From Nowhere became its official name. For 381 days, money like hers kept a parallel transit system running: hundreds of cars, dispatchers, pickup points, and drivers moving tens of thousands of people to work, every day, without the buses.[^1]

The standard account of the civil rights movement centres marches, speeches, and courtrooms. Underneath every one of those set pieces was a care system: someone fed the marchers, housed the organisers, taught the classes, and drove the workers. The historians who have worked closest to the ground, Charles Payne and Barbara Ransby among them, argue that this infrastructure was the movement's foundation rather than its support system, and that in long stretches it simply was the movement.[^2]

## Schools in Kitchens

In 1957, on Johns Island, South Carolina, Septima Clark and her cousin Bernice Robinson opened a school in the back room of a cooperative store, teaching Black adults to read and write using the documents of daily life: the Bible, mail-order catalogues, and the South Carolina voter registration form. The **citizenship schools** spread across the South, run by local people in kitchens, church basements, and beauty parlours. They taught literacy, and with it the machinery of civic life: how to register, how to fill out a money order, how to face a registrar. Hundreds of these schools operated by the mid-1960s, and the movement's field organisers repeatedly traced voter registration drives back to them.[^3]

The **Freedom Schools** of Mississippi's 1964 Summer Project ran on the same design at higher intensity: more than forty schools, roughly two thousand students, curriculum written by volunteers, housed in churches and on porches, feeding the children who came. The internal documents are explicit that the schools were community institutions first, providing meals, care, and a safe gathering place in counties where public institutions provided none of it to Black residents.[^4]

## Food as a Front

In the winter of 1962–63, Leflore County, Mississippi, cut off the federal surplus food programme on which some twenty thousand of its poorest residents depended, in retaliation for a voter registration drive in Greenwood. SNCC's response was a food programme of its own: tons of donated food, driven into the Delta from the North, distributed alongside registration canvassing. Feeding people and registering them became one operation, and the field reports from that winter read like the ledgers of a relief agency. The county had turned food into a weapon; the movement turned food into organising.[^5]

None of this was improvised from nothing. The field secretaries who moved through Mississippi were housed, fed, hidden, and protected by local families, at real risk to those families, in the pattern Payne calls the organising tradition: slow, relational work built on households rather than crowds. Ella Baker, who had spent the 1940s organising NAACP branches town by town, built SNCC deliberately on that model: group-centred leadership, starting with people where they are, and care for the daily needs of the community as the first organising act. Behind all of it stood the Black church, which had functioned as a mutual aid institution since the days of the Free African Society, and which now supplied the movement its buildings, its treasuries, its networks, and its people.[^6]

## The Same Tradition, Under Pressure

Read against the earlier pages of this archive, the civil rights movement is not a new story. It is the mutual aid tradition operating under maximum pressure: the same reciprocal provision that built the benevolent societies and the lodges, now carrying a political movement on its back. The marches lasted days. The care systems ran for years, and the people who ran them, disproportionately women, cooks, teachers, and church stewards, appear in the famous photographs mostly at the edge of the frame.

A decade after Montgomery, in Oakland, the same tradition would surface again in its most visible modern form: children eating breakfast before school, served by an organisation the federal government was simultaneously copying and destroying.
