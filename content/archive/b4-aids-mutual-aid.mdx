In January 1982, six men met in a New York living room and founded the **Gay Men's Health Crisis**, an organisation for an epidemic that did not yet have a name. Its first infrastructure was an answering machine, and the calls overwhelmed it immediately. Within months, GMHC had built a hotline staffed by volunteers, crisis counselling, and legal help for men who were losing their jobs and apartments, and it had created the **buddy programme**, which assigned a volunteer to each sick man to cook, clean, run errands, and stay present through an illness that made much of the world, including many hospitals and families, recoil.[^1]

The federal government's response to the deadliest epidemic of the century was, for years, close to nothing. Research went underfunded, prevention went unmentioned, and the President did not deliver a major address on AIDS until 1987, by which time more than twenty thousand Americans had died. The refusal had a logic, because the people dying were, in large majority, gay men, drug users, and the poor, whom the political culture had already placed outside its care. The record of that refusal is extensive and unambiguous.[^2]

What filled the void was mutual aid in the pattern that had recurred since 1787, in which an excluded community organises its own provision. The community built buddy teams, meal deliveries, food pantries that ran for decades, therapy and support groups, and housing funds, and it operated needle exchanges openly before they were legal, alongside community-run clinics and hospices. The care networks came first, years before the famous protests. When ACT UP formed in 1987 and put grief on the street as direct action, it grew out of rooms full of people who had been nursing, burying, and provisioning each other since the epidemic began. The historians of the movement are specific about this sequence, because the care was the foundation, and the politics rose from it.[^3]

## Care for Everyone the Crisis Touched

The networks crossed the lines that peacetime institutions treated as fixed. ACT UP's membership and the broader caregiving world around it included women and men, Black, Latino, and white members, the HIV-positive and the negative, and nurses and lawyers alongside people with no credentials except availability. Needle exchange extended the community's care to drug users whom even sympathetic institutions refused to touch. The organising principle was need and presence rather than category, and it was the same principle that had organised the fever year of 1793 and the Delta winter of 1963.[^4]

The movement also pooled something rarer than food or money, because its treatment activists turned expertise itself into mutual aid. They taught themselves virology and biostatistics, audited trial designs, and forced changes in how the FDA approved drugs, and their intervention in scientific practice is now studied in its own right. Knowledge, like food and nursing, was gathered and shared as community property.[^5]

## Keeping the Record

In 1794, Absalom Jones and Richard Allen published a narrative of what their community had actually done during Philadelphia's plague year, because the public record was already being falsified. Two centuries later, the AIDS generation did the same thing at scale, building oral history projects and organisational archives and publishing books whose titles state the purpose plainly, such as *Let the Record Show*. The tradition has learned, epidemic by epidemic, that care work disappears from history unless the people who did it write it down.[^6]

The buddy programmes and pantries sustained thousands of people through the years when no institution would, and some of that infrastructure still operates. What the epidemic proved, which is that a community abandoned by the state can build competent and durable care at speed, had been quietly true all along of the oldest and least recorded care system in the country, the networks that women ran in every era without anyone calling them an institution.
