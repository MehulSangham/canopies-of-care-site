In January 1969, in the basement of St. Augustine's Episcopal Church in Oakland, the Black Panther Party began serving free breakfast to school children. The programme spread through the party's chapters until kitchens in cities across the country were feeding thousands of children every school morning. The children ate eggs, grits, toast, and milk, served by party members and neighbourhood volunteers before the first bell.[^1]

![Black Panther free breakfast flyer, 1970](/images/b3/free-breakfast-1970.jpg "A Black Panther Party flyer for the Free Breakfast for School Children programme, Washington, DC, 1970. © Public domain, via Washington Area Spark and Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Black_Panther_Party_Free_Breakfast.jpg")

Breakfast was the most famous of what the party called its **survival programmes**, "survival pending revolution." There were free medical clinics in more than a dozen cities, staffed by volunteer doctors and nurses. There was free sickle cell anaemia testing, at a time when the disease was barely acknowledged by public health authorities, and the Panthers' campaign is a large part of why it became a national issue. There were free clothing and shoe programmes, free plumbing and pest control, escorts for the elderly, an ambulance service, and free busing to prisons so families could visit the incarcerated.[^2]

The programmes were mutual aid in the precise sense that the tradition had carried since 1787. They were organised by the community for the community, they ran on reciprocity rather than on eligibility tests, and the people running them understood them as civic infrastructure with political meaning. Scholars have been explicit on this point, placing the survival programmes in the direct line of the benevolent societies and the lodges, and describing them as the party's deepest connection to the neighbourhoods it served.[^3]

![Front page of The Black Panther newspaper, 1967](/images/b3/black-panther-paper-1967.jpg "The Black Panther, the party's newspaper, July 1967. The paper documented the survival programmes week by week, one of the reasons the record survives. © Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Black_Panther,_Vol._1,_No._4,_July_3,_1967.pdf")

## The Bureau's Assessment

The federal government agreed about the programmes' power, in its own way. In May 1969, J. Edgar Hoover sent a memo to every FBI field office:

> The BCP (Breakfast for Children Program) promotes at least tacit support for the Black Panther Party among naive individuals and, what is more distressing, it provides the BPP with a ready audience composed of highly impressionable youths. Consequently, the BCP represents the best and most influential activity going for the BPP and, as such, is potentially the greatest threat to efforts by authorities to neutralize the BPP and destroy what it stands for.

The breakfast programme, by the FBI's own analysis, was the greatest threat the party posed, greater than any armed patrol. COINTELPRO operations followed the analysis. Agents raided breakfast sites, spread rumours to parents and churches that the food was poisoned or that the programme was a front, and put pressure on the landlords and suppliers who made the kitchens possible. When one field office suggested that the breakfast programme might be left alone, Hoover rebuked it in writing: "You have obviously missed the point."[^4]

## Taken and Erased

While the Bureau worked to destroy the programme, the federal government scaled its model. The School Breakfast Program, a small pilot from 1966, was expanded through the early 1970s and made permanent in 1975, as the Panthers' kitchens were demonstrating, city by city, that feeding children before school was possible, cheap, and popular. The geographer Nik Heynen states the connection plainly: the Panther programme "was both the model for, and impetus behind, all federally funded school breakfast programs currently in existence within the United States."[^5]

It is tempting to call that a success, and in one sense it is, because millions of children now eat breakfast at school. But the adoption arrived paired with destruction. The organisation that proved the model was raided, infiltrated, and dismantled through the same years in which the model was being written into federal law. The practice was taken, the politics were stripped out of it, and the community that built it was erased from the story, so that almost no one who encounters the School Breakfast Program today learns where it came from.[^6]

The sequence deserves to be stated as a mechanism, because it is the same one that ran through the New Deal decades. The displacement of mutual aid is not always neglect. Sometimes the state absorbs the function and deletes the author. The New Deal did this to the lodges over the course of a generation, and COINTELPRO and the school breakfast expansion did it to the Panthers inside a single decade, leaving the paperwork behind as proof.

The survival programmes were gone by 1980. Within two years, the tradition met a different kind of official failure, when an epidemic arrived that the federal government declined, for years, to answer, and the communities it struck had to build the response themselves.
